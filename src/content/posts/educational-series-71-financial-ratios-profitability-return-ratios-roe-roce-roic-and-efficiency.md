Welcome to **Lesson 71** of **Module 03: Fundamental Analysis** (**Lessons 61–90**) in the **Shree Fanidhar Capital Educational Series**.

Raw rupee numbers on their own can be hard to compare across years or competitors of different sizes—so analysts use **Financial Ratios** to standardize performance, measure capital productivity, and test operational efficiency.

---

## 1. Return Ratios: How Effectively Capital Is Compounded

**Return ratios** compare profit generated against the capital base used to produce it. Over the long run, a stock’s compounding potential is closely tied to how high a return the underlying business earns on incremental capital (`ROIC` / `ROCE`) and how much of those profits it reinvests.

| Return Ratio | Formula | What It Measures | Key Nuance for Analysts |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Return on Equity (`ROE`)** | $\frac{\text{Profit After Tax (PAT)}}{\text{Average Shareholders’ Equity}} \times 100$ | Return earned for **equity shareholders** on their net worth. | Can be artificially boosted by high debt (`financial leverage`) or shrinking equity from large buybacks. |
| **Return on Capital Employed (`ROCE`)** | $\frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Capital Employed}} \times 100$ | Pre-tax operating return on **all long-term capital** (`Net Worth + Total Debt`, or `Total Assets − Current Liabilities`). | Widely used in Indian equity research to compare businesses across different debt structures. |
| **Return on Invested Capital (`ROIC`)** | $\frac{\text{NOPAT}}{\text{Invested Capital}} \times 100$ | After-tax operating return (`NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate)`) on **core operating capital** (`Equity + Debt − Cash & Non-Operating Investments`). | Best measure of **true economic moat & operational capital productivity** because excess cash does not dilute the denominator. |

> **Rule of Thumb:** In non-financial businesses, sustained `ROCE` and `ROIC` consistently above **`15%–18%`** (well above a typical `10%–12%` cost of equity/capital) usually point to a strong competitive position and disciplined capital use.

## 2. Profitability & Margin Ratios: Pricing Power & Cost Control

While return ratios judge capital efficiency, **margin ratios** show how much of every rupee of revenue survives at each stage of the `P&L`:

* **Gross Margin:** $\frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Revenue from Operations}} \times 100$ — Shows direct product pricing power and raw-material cost cushion.
* **Operating Margin (`EBITDA` Margin):** $\frac{\text{EBITDA}}{\text{Revenue from Operations}} \times 100$ — Shows core operating efficiency across manufacturing, staff, and overhead costs.
* **Net Profit Margin (`PAT` Margin):** $\frac{\text{PAT}}{\text{Revenue from Operations}} \times 100$ — Shows final bottom-line conversion after depreciation, interest, taxes, and other income.

Always track margins across a **5-to-8-year cycle** rather than a single peak year—commodities and cyclical sectors see margins swing sharply up and down.

## 3. Efficiency & Turnover Ratios: Sweating the Asset Base

**Efficiency (activity) ratios** measure how quickly a business converts balance-sheet assets into sales or cash:

| Efficiency Ratio | Formula | What It Measures | Analytical Interpretation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Total Asset Turnover** | $\frac{\text{Revenue from Operations}}{\text{Average Total Assets}}$ | How much revenue each rupee of assets generates. | High in asset-light retail/FMCG/services; lower in capital-heavy steel, power, or infrastructure. |
| **Inventory Turnover** | $\frac{\text{COGS}}{\text{Average Inventory}}$ | How many times inventory cycles through per year. | Higher turnover means faster stock movement and lower risk of obsolescence. |
| **Receivables Turnover** | $\frac{\text{Revenue from Operations}}{\text{Average Trade Receivables}}$ | How quickly credit sales are converted into receivables/cash. | Falling turnover signals slower collections or stretched credit terms. |

## 4. Worked Example: Spotting a High-`ROE` Illusion (`₹ Crore`)

Suppose **Company X** and **Company Y** both generate **`₹90 Cr` in `PAT`**, but their capital structures differ sharply:

| Metric | Company X *(Low Debt, Net Cash)* | Company Y *(Heavy Debt)* | Takeaway |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **`PAT`** | `₹90` | `₹90` | Same net profit |
| **Shareholders’ Equity (`Net Worth`)** | `₹600` | `₹300` | Company Y has half the equity base |
| **Total Debt** | `₹20` | `₹500` | Company Y relies heavily on borrowings |
| **`ROE` (`PAT / Equity`)** | **`15.0%`** | **`30.0%`** | Company Y looks double on `ROE` due to leverage |
| **`ROCE` (`EBIT / Capital Employed`)** | **`18.5%`** | **`11.2%`** | True operating return is stronger in Company X |

**Lesson:** Never celebrate a high `ROE` blindly—always check **Debt-to-Equity**, **Interest Coverage**, and **`ROIC` / `ROCE`** alongside it.

---

## 5. Common Traps When Using Financial Ratios

1. **Comparing Ratios Across Different Sectors:** A `1.8x` asset turnover may be weak in trading/FMCG yet strong in heavy infrastructure. Always compare peers within the **same industry**.
2. **Ignoring Cash Pile Distortions in `ROCE`:** A company sitting on huge cash reserves (`₹2,000 Cr` cash on a `₹3,000 Cr` balance sheet) earns low interest on that cash, pulling `ROCE` down even when its core operating business earns `35%+`. Checking **`ROIC`** (excluding excess cash) removes that distortion.
3. **One-Time Gains or Write-Offs Skewing Numerators:** A single non-recurring gain or tax adjustment can temporarily inflate `ROE` or `Net Margin` for one quarter or year.

---

## Key Takeaways

* **Return ratios (`ROE`, `ROCE`, `ROIC`) measure capital productivity**, with **`ROIC` and `ROCE`** offering cleaner views of operating business quality than `ROE` alone.
* **Margin ratios (`Gross`, `EBITDA`, `Net`) reveal pricing power and cost discipline**, best read over multi-year cycles.
* **Efficiency ratios (`Asset`, `Inventory`, `Receivables` turnover)** show how hard the balance sheet works to generate sales—always interpret them alongside debt and sector norms.
