---

## 1. Standalone vs. Consolidated Statements

* **Standalone Financial Statements:** Reflect only the **single parent legal entity** as an independent unit. Inter-company transactions with subsidiaries or group entities appear as normal revenues, receivables, loans, or dividend income.
* **Consolidated Financial Statements:** Combine the financial results of the **parent company and all controlled subsidiaries** (`100%` line-by-line consolidation), eliminating intra-group transactions so you see the economic group as one unified enterprise.

| Feature | Standalone Statements | Consolidated Statements |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Scope** | Parent entity alone | Parent + all controlled subsidiaries |
| **Intra-Group Transactions** | Included (e.g., dividends from subsidiaries) | Eliminated to avoid double counting |
| **Minority Share of Profit/Equity** | Not applicable | Shown separately as **Non-Controlling Interest (`NCI`)** |
| **Primary Analytical Use** | Checking parent solvency, dividend capacity & tax/regulatory filings | Assessing true overall group earnings, scale & consolidated debt |

---

## 2. How Subsidiaries, Associates & Joint Ventures Are Accounted For (`Ind AS`)

Under Indian Accounting Standards (`Ind AS`), group entities are treated differently depending on the degree of **control** or **significant influence**:

1. **Subsidiaries (Control — typically `> 50%` voting power or control over board/decisions):**
   * Consolidated **line by line** (`100%` of revenue, assets, liabilities, and expenses) in the Consolidated P&L and Balance Sheet.
   * If the parent owns less than `100%` (say `75%`), the remaining `25%` outside share of net profit and net assets is carved out as **Non-Controlling Interest (`NCI`)**.
2. **Associates (Significant Influence — typically `20%–50%` voting power):**
   * Accounted for using the **Equity Method**: the parent's share of the associate's net profit/loss appears as a **single line item** (`Share of profit of associates`) in the Consolidated P&L, and the carrying investment value adjusts on the Balance Sheet.
3. **Joint Ventures (`JVs` — joint control and shared rights to net assets):**
   * Also accounted for using the **Equity Method** in Consolidated financial statements (`Share of profit of joint ventures`).

---

## 3. Why Comparing Both Standalone and Consolidated Matters

Relying exclusively on only one view can blind you to important risks or hidden strengths:

* **Debt Tucked Inside Subsidiaries:** A parent company may show low standalone debt while an overseas or project subsidiary carries heavy borrowings or losses.
* **Dividends vs. Real Cash at the Parent:** A subsidiary can report healthy profits on paper while cash stays trapped abroad or inside a subsidiary—meaning the parent can only upstream cash via dividends/management fees subject to local rules and taxes.
* **Loss-Making Subsidiaries Dragging Consolidated `PAT`:** A strong standalone core business can have consolidated profits pulled down while a new subsidiary scales up—or vice versa, where a fast-growing subsidiary drives the bulk of group growth.

---

## Key Takeaways

* **Start with Consolidated statements** as your primary lens when a company has active subsidiaries, then **check Standalone statements** to see where cash, debt, and dividend-paying capacity sit.
* **Subsidiaries are consolidated line by line** (with Non-Controlling Interest separated when ownership is below `100%`), while **Associates and Joint Ventures** typically appear via the **equity method** as a single profit line.
* **Always inspect subsidiary footnotes** for hidden debt, contingent guarantees, or loss-making units that don't show up on the standalone balance sheet alone.
