Welcome to **Lesson 86** of **Module 03: Fundamental Analysis** (**Lessons 61–90**) in the **Shree Fanidhar Capital Educational Series**.

In **Lesson 85**, we covered **Discounted Cash Flow (`DCF`) Foundations** (`Time Value of Money`, `WACC`, and `Terminal Value`). Across **Lessons 82–85**, you have seen two broad families of valuation tools: **Relative Valuation** (`P/E`, `PEG`, `P/B`, `P/S`, `EV/EBITDA`) and **Intrinsic Valuation** (`DCF`). Today, we compare how these two approaches work together—and why **Margin of Safety** is essential when turning any valuation estimate into an investment decision.

---

## 1. Relative Valuation vs. Intrinsic Valuation

| Dimension | Relative Valuation (`Multiples`) | Intrinsic Valuation (`DCF` / Asset Worth) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Core Question** | How is the market pricing this stock relative to **peers or its own history**? | What is the business worth based on **future cash flows, `ROIC`, and risk**? |
| **Primary Inputs** | Market price/cap, `EPS`, book value, sales, `EBITDA`, peer/historical multiples. | Projected `FCF`, growth rates, `WACC` / required return, terminal assumptions. |
| **Strengths** | Fast, grounded in current market pricing, useful for sanity-checking expectations. | Forces you to model business economics, reinvestment, and cash generation explicitly. |
| **Key Risk / Trap** | **The "Cheap Peer" Trap:** An entire sector or peer group can be overvalued together—or a low multiple may reflect real structural weakness. | **False Precision:** Small shifts in growth or discount rates can swing estimated value widely if assumptions are rosy. |

---

## 2. Peer Benchmarking: Making Multiples Meaningful

Comparing a stock's multiple against peers or its 5-to-10-year median only works when you adjust for **fundamental differences**:

1. **Growth & Return Spreads:** A company compounding `EPS` at `22%` with `24%` `ROIC` and net cash can rationally trade at a higher `P/E` or `EV/EBITDA` than a slower peer growing `9%` with `12%` `ROIC` and rising debt—even if the slower peer looks "cheaper" at a glance.
2. **Cyclicality & Accounting Differences:** Compare like with like (`Consolidated` vs. `Consolidated`, `EBITDA` vs. `EBIT`, consistent treatment of non-recurring items and lease/other-income lines).
3. **Why Historical Multiples Shift:** A company's average multiple over the past five years may not hold forever if its growth matures, competition intensifies, or capital intensity rises.

---

## 3. Margin of Safety: Building Cushion for the Unexpected

Because **every forecast is imperfect**—macro shifts, raw-material swings, execution delays, or competitive pressure can happen—**Margin of Safety** is the discount between your conservative **estimated intrinsic value** and the **price you actually pay**:

$$\text{Margin of Safety (\%)} = \frac{\text{Estimated Intrinsic Value} - \text{Purchase Price}}{\text{Estimated Intrinsic Value}} \times 100$$

* Suppose your conservative intrinsic value estimate for a business is **`₹1,000` per share**, and you buy at **`₹750` per share**—that gives a **`25%` Margin of Safety** (`(1,000 − 750) / 1,000`).
* **Higher Uncertainty Requires a Larger Cushion:** Small-cap, cyclical, or turnaround businesses with wider outcome ranges warrant a wider margin of safety than steady, high-visibility compounders—and no margin of safety rescues a business with poor governance or severe balance-sheet distress (`Lessons 73 & 76`).

---

## Key Takeaways

* **Relative valuation** benchmarks a stock against peers or history; **intrinsic valuation** anchors value to cash flows and business economics. Using both prevents blind spots.
* **Peer comparisons must account for growth, `ROIC`, balance-sheet strength, and cyclicality** rather than treating the lowest multiple as automatically cheap.
* **Margin of Safety** bridges the gap between analytical estimates and real-world uncertainty—helping protect capital when assumptions face headwinds.
