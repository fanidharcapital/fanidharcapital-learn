## 1. Price-to-Earnings (`P/E`) and Earnings Yield (`E/P`)

The **Price-to-Earnings (`P/E`)** ratio tells you how many rupees investors are currently paying for `₹1` of annual earnings (`EPS`):

$$\text{P/E Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Price per Share}}{\text{Earnings per Share (EPS)}} = \frac{\text{Market Capitalization}}{\text{Total Profit After Tax (PAT)}}$$

* **Trailing `P/E` (`TTM P/E`):** Uses reported `EPS` over the trailing 12 months (`4 quarters`).
* **Forward `P/E`:** Uses estimated future `EPS` (typically next `12` months or next fiscal year)—useful when earnings are inflecting, but only as reliable as the underlying forecast.

### Inverting `P/E` Into Earnings Yield
Flipping `P/E` upside down gives **Earnings Yield (`E/P`)**, expressed as a percentage:

$$\text{Earnings Yield (\%)} = \frac{\text{EPS}}{\text{Market Price per Share}} \times 100 = \frac{1}{\text{P/E}} \times 100$$

* A stock trading at **`25x P/E`** has an **Earnings Yield of `4.0%`** (`1 / 25 = 0.04`).
* A stock trading at **`12.5x P/E`** has an **Earnings Yield of `8.0%`** (`1 / 12.5 = 0.08`).

Comparing **Earnings Yield** alongside prevailing benchmark yields (such as Indian government bond yields or bank fixed-deposit rates) helps frame the *opportunity cost* of holding equities—though remember that a bond coupon is contractual while equity earnings can grow (or shrink) over time.

---

## 2. The `PEG` Ratio: Adjusting `P/E` for Growth

A `30x P/E` stock growing earnings at `28%` annually is very different from a `30x P/E` stock growing earnings at `6%`. The **Price/Earnings-to-Growth (`PEG`)** ratio divides `P/E` by expected annual earnings growth rate (`g`, in percentage points):

$$\text{PEG Ratio} = \frac{\text{P/E Ratio}}{\text{Expected Annual EPS Growth Rate (g in \%)}}$$

| Company | `P/E` | Expected Annual `EPS` Growth (`g`) | `PEG Ratio` (`P/E ÷ g`) | Quick Interpretation |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **Company A (`Fast Compounder`)** | `30x` | `25%` (`25`) | **`1.20`** | Higher `P/E`, but backed by strong `25%` growth |
| **Company B (`Moderate Grower`)** | `22x` | `12%` (`12`) | **`1.83`** | Moderate multiple, but slower growth relative to price paid |
| **Company C (`Cyclical / Low Growth`)** | `12x` | `15%` (`15`) | **`0.80`** | Low `PEG`—verify whether `15%` growth is sustainable or a cyclical peak |

---

## 3. Practical Guardrails When Using `P/E` and `PEG`

1. **Never Use `P/E` Alone on Cyclicals:** Near a cyclical peak, commodity/industrial profits (`EPS`) are abnormally high and `P/E` looks deceptively low (`7x–9x`) right before earnings fall. Near a trough, `EPS` is depressed and `P/E` looks optically expensive (`40x+`) right before recovery.
2. **Watch `EPS` Quality Behind the Multiple:** A low `P/E` caused by **one-off Other Income**, tax write-backs, or aggressive accrual accounting can be a trap. Always check `CFO`, core `OPM`, and `ROIC` (`Lessons 74–78`).
3. **Treat `PEG` as a Heuristic, Not a Formulaic Law:** `PEG` assumes a linear relationship between growth and valuation multiple, ignores `ROIC` and capital intensity (a business growing at `20%` by diluting equity or taking heavy debt is worth less than one compounding at `20%` with high `ROIC`), and relies on future growth assumptions (`g`).

---

## Key Takeaways

* **`P/E` measures price paid per rupee of `EPS`**, while **Earnings Yield (`1 / P/E`)** converts that multiple into a percentage yield comparable against interest-rate benchmarks.
* **The `PEG` Ratio (`P/E ÷ Growth %`)** helps contextualize valuation against expected `EPS` growth, but must always be paired with business quality, `ROIC`, and cash-flow checks.
* **Always inspect `EPS` durability and cyclicality** before concluding a stock is cheap or expensive on `P/E` alone.
