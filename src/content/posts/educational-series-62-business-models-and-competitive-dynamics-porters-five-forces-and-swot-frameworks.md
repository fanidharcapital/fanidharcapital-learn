Welcome to **Lesson 62** of **Module 03: Fundamental Analysis** (**Lessons 61–90**) in the **Shree Fanidhar Capital Educational Series**.

In **Lesson 61**, we established that fundamental analysis seeks a business's **Intrinsic Value** by combining quantitative numbers with qualitative reality. Before diving into balance sheets and financial ratios, a disciplined analyst answers a simpler, foundational question: **How does this company actually make money, and what stops competitors from taking its profits?**

---

## 1. Deconstructing a Business Model

A **Business Model** is the blueprint of how an enterprise creates, delivers, and captures economic value. When studying any listed Indian company—from an FMCG giant to an IT exporter or specialty chemical manufacturer—break its operations into four building blocks:

1. **Value Proposition & Customer Segment:** What core problem does the product or service solve, and who pays for it (`B2C` retail consumers, `B2B` enterprises, or government institutions)?
2. **Revenue Engine & Pricing Power:** Does the company earn from one-time product sales, recurring subscriptions/AMCs, transaction fees, or long-term contracts? Can it pass on raw material inflation to customers without losing volume?
3. **Cost Structure & Operating Leverage:** Are costs predominantly variable (raw materials, packaging, freight) or fixed (manufacturing plants, R&D, software platforms)? High fixed-cost businesses see rapid margin expansion once revenue crosses break-even—and sharp margin compression during slowdowns.
4. **Capital Intensity & Working Capital Cycle:** How much machinery, inventory, or customer credit (`receivables`) is required to generate ₹1 of incremental sales?

> **Analytical Rule:** Never judge a company by its brand familiarity alone. Two companies in the same sector can have vastly different economics—for instance, a **franchised / asset-light** retail brand generates high return on capital with minimal debt, while a **company-owned / asset-heavy** operator ties up substantial cash in real estate and inventory.

## 2. Porter’s Five Forces: Measuring Industry Attractiveness

Developed by Harvard strategist **Michael Porter**, the **Five Forces Framework** evaluates why certain industries consistently earn high returns on capital (such as branded consumer staples, exchanges, and rating agencies) while others struggle with razor-thin margins (such as airlines or unbranded commodities).

| Competitive Force | Core Question for the Analyst | High-Force Warning Sign (Margin Pressure) | Low-Force Advantage (Profit Protection) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Threat of New Entrants** | How easily can a well-funded newcomer enter this market? | Low capital requirements, simple technology, minimal regulatory approvals. | High capex barriers, strict licensing (SEBI/RBI/pharma approvals), strong distribution networks, or deep brand trust. |
| **2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers** | Can key input providers squeeze the company's margins? | Few concentrated suppliers, specialized raw materials, high switching costs. | Fragmented supplier base, standardized inputs, or backward integration by the company. |
| **3. Bargaining Power of Buyers** | Can customers demand lower prices or longer credit periods? | A few large B2B clients account for most revenue; products are undifferentiated. | Millions of dispersed retail consumers or mission-critical products with high switching costs. |
| **4. Threat of Substitutes** | Can an alternative product fulfill the same customer need? | Cheaper or superior alternatives exist with zero switching friction. | No close substitute exists, or switching disrupts customer workflows. |
| **5. Rivalry Among Existing Competitors** | How fiercely do current industry players fight for market share? | Slow industry growth, excess capacity, price wars, and identical offerings. | Rational oligopoly pricing, differentiated products, and steady demand expansion. |

## 3. SWOT Analysis: Mapping Internal Capabilities vs. External Reality

While **Porter’s Five Forces** examines the *entire industry*, **SWOT Analysis** zooms in on a **specific company's positioning** by separating internal execution from external environment:

* **Strengths *(Internal · Positive)*:** Proprietary technology, low-cost manufacturing scale, pan-India distribution reach, net-cash balance sheet, or sticky customer relationships.
* **Weaknesses *(Internal · Negative)*:** High customer or geographic concentration, elevated debt, aging plant infrastructure, or weak working-capital discipline.
* **Opportunities *(External · Positive)*:** Export market share gains (`China+1`), formalization of unorganized sectors, favorable PLI/policy incentives, or premiumization trends.
* **Threats *(External · Negative)*:** Volatile commodity prices, regulatory price caps, technological disruption, or aggressive entry by deep-pocketed conglomerates.

| Dimension | Helpful to Achieving Objectives | Harmful to Achieving Objectives |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Internal Origin** *(Management Controls)* | **Strengths** *(Build & compound)* | **Weaknesses** *(Fix or mitigate)* |
| **External Origin** *(Industry & Macro)* | **Opportunities** *(Capture & scale)* | **Threats** *(Monitor & hedge)* |

## 4. Putting Both Frameworks Together in Practice

When evaluating a stock, use both tools in sequence rather than isolation:

1. **Start with the Business Model:** Confirm how revenue is earned, how cash flows through working capital, and whether the company has pricing power.
2. **Apply Porter’s Five Forces:** Determine whether the industry structure allows participants to retain profits or forces them to pass every efficiency gain away to buyers and suppliers.
3. **Run a Sharp, Evidence-Backed SWOT:** Avoid vague statements like *"good management"* or *"big market."* Tie every bullet to verifiable evidence—for example, *"Strength: 42% EBITDA margin backed by backward-integrated intermediates"* or *"Threat: Top 3 clients contribute 58% of revenue."*

---

## Key Takeaways

* **A business model explains how an enterprise creates and captures cash**, linking value proposition, pricing power, cost structure, and capital intensity.
* **Porter’s Five Forces measures industry-wide profit durability** across new entrants, suppliers, buyers, substitutes, and existing rivalry.
* **SWOT Analysis separates internal capabilities (`Strengths`, `Weaknesses`) from external market forces (`Opportunities`, `Threats`)**, turning qualitative observations into structured research checklists.
