Welcome to **Lesson 83** of **Module 03: Fundamental Analysis** (**Lessons 61–90**) in the **Shree Fanidhar Capital Educational Series**.

In **Lesson 82**, we covered **Price-to-Earnings (`P/E`)**, **Earnings Yield (`E/P`)**, and the **`PEG` Ratio**. Sometimes `P/E` breaks down or tells an incomplete story—such as when a company has temporary losses, volatile earnings, or a balance sheet whose equity value is the real engine of profits. Today, we examine **Price-to-Book (`P/B`)** and **Price-to-Sales (`P/S`)**.

---

## 1. Price-to-Book (`P/B`) Ratio

**Book Value per Share (`BVPS`)** represents net accounting equity (`Shareholders' Equity` divided by total shares). The **Price-to-Book (`P/B`)** ratio compares market valuation against that accounting equity base:

$$\text{P/B Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Price per Share}}{\text{Book Value per Share (BVPS)}} = \frac{\text{Market Capitalization}}{\text{Total Shareholders’ Equity}}$$

### Connecting `P/B` to `ROE`
Just as `P/E` relates to earnings growth, justified `P/B` is closely tied to **Return on Equity (`ROE`)** and cost of equity:
* A bank or lender earning **`17%` `ROE`** sustainably with strong asset quality can generally command a higher `P/B` multiple than a peer earning **`9%` `ROE`** with weaker underwriting.
* A low `P/B` (`< 1.0x`) on a bank or asset-heavy company can reflect either **undervaluation** or **underlying asset stress** (such as unprovided bad loans or impaired machinery).

---

## 2. Price-to-Sales (`P/S`) Ratio

The **Price-to-Sales (`P/S`)** ratio measures how much investors pay per rupee of revenue:

$$\text{P/S Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Capitalization}}{\text{Total Revenue from Operations}} = \frac{\text{Market Price per Share}}{\text{Sales per Share}}$$

### Why `P/S` Is Used—and Its Biggest Blind Spot
* **Where `P/S` Helps:** Useful when a business is temporarily loss-making (`negative P/E`), reinvesting heavily for market share, or going through a margin trough—provided you believe margins will normalize over time.
* **The Blind Spot:** **Revenue is not profit.** Two companies trading at **`3x P/S`** look identical on sales multiple, but one with a **`22%` net margin** (`~13.6x P/E`) has vastly stronger earning power than one with a **`4%` net margin** (`75x P/E`) or weak cash collections.

---

## 3. Side-by-Side Comparison: `P/B` vs. `P/S` vs. `P/E`

| Valuation Metric | Best Suited For | Key Strength | Key Limitation / Trap |
| :--- | :---: | :--- | :--- |
| **`P/E` Ratio** | Profitable companies with stable/recurring earnings | Directly links price to bottom-line `EPS` | Distorted by cyclical earnings swings, one-offs, or losses |
| **`P/B` Ratio** | Banks, NBFCs, insurers, and asset-heavy balance sheets | Anchors valuation to net equity / tangible asset base | Ignores off-balance-sheet brand/IP value and true asset quality |
| **`P/S` Ratio** | High-growth / margin-inflecting / temporarily loss-making firms | Revenue is harder to wipe out with single-year accounting losses | Completely ignores cost structure, margins, debt, and cash flow |

---

## 4. Practical Guardrails When Using `P/B` and `P/S`

1. **Check Asset Quality Before Trusting Low `P/B`:** For banks and NBFCs, verify `GNPA / NNPA`, provisioning coverage, and capital adequacy (`Lesson 80`). For industrials, check whether book assets are productive or idle/obsolete.
2. **Pair `P/S` With Target Margins & Cash Flow:** Always ask: *At a realistic `OPM` and `NPM` for this industry, what implied `P/E` does this `P/S` multiple represent?*
3. **Avoid Mixing `P/B` Across Incompatible Sectors:** Comparing a capital-light tech/services firm's `P/B` against a bank's `P/B` is rarely meaningful because their balance-sheet structures are fundamentally different.

---

## Key Takeaways

* **`P/B` compares market value to shareholders' equity**, making it especially relevant for financials and asset-backed businesses when paired with **`ROE`** and asset-quality checks.
* **`P/S` compares market value to revenue**, making it useful when earnings are depressed or negative—but **never ignore profit margins, debt, and cash flow** behind the top line.
* **Match the multiple to the business model** rather than forcing every stock through a single lens.
