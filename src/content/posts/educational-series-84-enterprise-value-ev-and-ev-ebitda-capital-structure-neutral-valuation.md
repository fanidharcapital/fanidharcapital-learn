Welcome to **Lesson 84** of **Module 03: Fundamental Analysis** (**Lessons 61–90**) in the **Shree Fanidhar Capital Educational Series**.

In **Lesson 83**, we covered **Price-to-Book (`P/B`)** and **Price-to-Sales (`P/S`)**. When comparing two businesses that finance themselves differently—one heavily debt-funded and one debt-free with ample cash—`P/E` and `Market Cap` alone can distort who is actually cheaper. Today, we examine **Enterprise Value (`EV`)** and the **`EV / EBITDA`** multiple.

---

## 1. What Is Enterprise Value (`EV`)?

**Market Capitalization** measures only the market value of **equity shares**. **Enterprise Value (`EV`)** estimates the total economic value of the **entire operating business**—as if an acquirer bought the equity, assumed the debt, and kept the available cash on the balance sheet:

$$\text{Enterprise Value (EV)} = \text{Market Cap} + \text{Total Debt} + \text{Non-Controlling Interest (NCI)} + \text{Preference Capital} - \text{Cash \& Cash Equivalents}$$

*(In everyday screening, analysts often use $\text{EV} \approx \text{Market Cap} + \text{Net Debt}$, where $\text{Net Debt} = \text{Total Debt} - \text{Cash \& Liquid Investments}$.)*

### Why Subtract Cash and Add Debt?
* **Debt Is Added:** If you buy a company with `₹500 Cr` of debt, you effectively inherit that obligation alongside the business.
* **Cash Is Subtracted:** If the business holds `₹200 Cr` of unrestricted cash, part of your purchase price is immediately offset by cash sitting in the till.

---

## 2. The `EV / EBITDA` Multiple

Dividing **Enterprise Value (`EV`)** by **`EBITDA`** (`Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization`) compares total enterprise value against operating cash-profit scale *before* financing structure, tax rates, and non-cash depreciation:

$$\text{EV / EBITDA} = \frac{\text{Enterprise Value (EV)}}{\text{EBITDA}}$$

### Worked Comparison: Same `EBITDA` (`₹200 Cr`), Different Capital Structures

Suppose **Alpha Steel & Alloys** and **Beta Steel & Alloys** both generate **`₹200 Cr`** in annual `EBITDA` and trade at a **`₹1,400 Cr` Market Cap**:

| Metric | Alpha (`Low Debt, High Cash`) | Beta (`High Debt, Low Cash`) | Analytical Takeaway |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **Market Capitalization** | `₹1,400 Cr` | `₹1,400 Cr` | Identical equity market cap (`P/E` may look similar) |
| **Total Debt** | `₹150 Cr` | `₹900 Cr` | Beta carries heavy borrowings |
| **Cash & Cash Equivalents** | `₹250 Cr` | `₹100 Cr` | Alpha holds net cash (`−₹100 Cr` net debt) |
| **Enterprise Value (`EV`)** | **`₹1,300 Cr`** (`1,400 − 100`) | **`₹2,200 Cr`** (`1,400 + 800`) | Total enterprise cost is `₹900 Cr` higher for Beta |
| **`EBITDA`** | `₹200 Cr` | `₹200 Cr` | Identical operating `EBITDA` |
| **`EV / EBITDA` Multiple** | **`6.5x`** | **`11.0x`** | Beta is far more expensive on a total-enterprise basis |

## 3. Practical Guardrails When Using `EV / EBITDA`

`EV / EBITDA` is widely used in corporate finance, M&A, and capital-intensive sectors—but it has important limitations you must check:

1. **`EBITDA` Ignores Maintenance `Capex`:** Two companies with the same `EBITDA` can have very different ongoing `Capex` needs. A low `EV / EBITDA` multiple on a heavy-capex business can still leave modest `Free Cash Flow` (`Lesson 71`).
2. **Watch Non-Cash or Aggressive Adjustments:** Always verify `EBITDA` against `Cash Flow from Operations (`CFO`)` (`Lesson 78`) and check whether operating expenses are being artificially shifted or capitalized.
3. **Not Ideal for Banks & NBFCs:** For lenders, debt and deposits are core operating raw materials rather than financing overhead—use sector-appropriate metrics like `P/B`, `ROE`, `NIM`, and `GNPA/NNPA` (`Lessons 80 & 83`).

---

## Key Takeaways

* **Enterprise Value (`EV`)** reflects the total value of the operating enterprise by accounting for **debt, minority/preference claims, and cash**.
* **`EV / EBITDA` is capital-structure-neutral**, making it useful for comparing companies with different debt and cash profiles across capital-intensive or M&A-active sectors.
* **Always pair `EV / EBITDA` with `Capex` intensity and `CFO` conversion** so high depreciation or heavy capital spending doesn't make an expensive business look cheap.
