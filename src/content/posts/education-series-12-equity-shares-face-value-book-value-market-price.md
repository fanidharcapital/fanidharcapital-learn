When you look up an equity share in India, you will encounter three completely different rupee numbers attached to the exact same stock: **Face Value**, **Book Value**, and **Market Price**.

For example, a company's share might have a **Face Value of ₹2**, a **Book Value of ₹180**, and a **Market Price of ₹1,250**. Understanding what each figure measures—and where each one matters—prevents common mistakes when reading dividends, stock splits, and company valuations.

---

## 1. Face Value (Par Value)

**Face Value** (also called **Par Value**) is the nominal value assigned to each share by the company in its Memorandum of Association when the share is originally issued.

* **Common Denominations in India:** Most listed Indian companies have a Face Value of **₹10, ₹5, ₹2, or ₹1** per share.
* **Does Face Value change with market demand?** **No.** Market rallies or crashes never alter Face Value. It changes *only* when the company carries out a **Stock Split** or **Share Consolidation**.
* **Why Face Value Matters:**
  1. **Dividend Declarations:** Companies often declare dividends as a percentage of Face Value. For instance, a **"200% dividend"** on a share with a **₹5 Face Value** equals **₹10 per share** (`200% × ₹5`)—*not* 200% of the Market Price.
  2. **Stock Splits:** If a ₹10 Face Value stock undergoes a **1:5 stock split**, each share splits into 5 shares with a new Face Value of **₹2** each. Your total investment value stays identical.
  3. **Share Capital Accounting:** Total Paid-up Equity Share Capital on the balance sheet equals `Face Value × Total Number of Shares`.

---

## 2. Book Value (Accounting Net Worth per Share)

**Book Value** represents the theoretical accounting value that equity shareholders would receive if the company sold all its assets at balance-sheet values and paid off all its liabilities.

$$\text{Total Book Value (Net Worth)} = \text{Total Assets} - \text{Total Liabilities}$$

$$\text{Book Value per Share (BVPS)} = \frac{\text{Shareholders' Equity (Share Capital + Reserves \& Surplus)}}{\text{Total Outstanding Equity Shares}}$$

* **What Drives Book Value:** Every year, the profit a company retains (after paying dividends) flows into **Reserves & Surplus**, steadily increasing its Book Value. Conversely, net losses erode Book Value.
* **Price-to-Book Ratio (P/B):** Investors compare Market Price against Book Value using the **P/B Ratio** (`Market Price ÷ Book Value per Share`):
  * **P/B > 1:** The market values the business above its accounting net worth due to brand strength, high Return on Equity (ROE), and future growth expectations.
  * **P/B < 1:** The stock trades below its accounting net worth—common in cyclical industries, capital-heavy businesses, or companies facing earnings stress.
* **Limitations:** Book Value relies on historical accounting costs rather than future earning power, making it far more useful for **banks, NBFCs, and asset-heavy companies** than for asset-light IT or consumer brands.

---

## 3. Market Price (Live Supply & Demand Value)

**Market Price** (seen on your broker screen as **LTP**) is the live price determined every second on NSE and BSE by buyers and sellers.

* **What Drives Market Price:** Future earnings expectations, revenue growth, interest rates, sector sentiment, institutional flows (FPI/DII), and macroeconomic news.
* **Forward-Looking vs. Backward-Looking:** While Book Value reflects *past accumulated accounting profits*, Market Price reflects *what investors believe the company will earn in the future*.

---

## 4. Side-by-Side Comparison

| Dimension | Face Value | Book Value | Market Price |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | Original nominal value fixed by the company at issuance. | Accounting net worth per share (`Assets − Liabilities ÷ Shares`). | Live trading price on NSE/BSE driven by supply and demand. |
| **Where to Find It** | Corporate info / Share capital section of annual report. | Balance sheet (Share Capital + Reserves ÷ Outstanding Shares). | Live stock quote / Broker app (LTP). |
| **How Often It Changes** | Rarely (only during a stock split or consolidation). | Quarterly / Annually as profits, losses, or buybacks occur. | Every second during market hours (9:15 AM – 3:30 PM IST). |
| **Primary Use Case** | Calculating percentage dividends and stock split ratios. | Evaluating banking/financial stocks and P/B valuation. | Buying/selling shares and calculating Market Capitalization. |

---

## 5. How a Single Example Connects All Three

Imagine **ABC Manufacturing Ltd.** has **10 crore** outstanding shares:
1. **Face Value = ₹10:** Its Equity Share Capital on the balance sheet is `10 crore × ₹10 = ₹100 crore`.
2. **Book Value = ₹120:** Over the years, it accumulated `₹1,100 crore` in Reserves & Surplus. Total Net Worth = `₹100 crore + ₹1,100 crore = ₹1,200 crore` → Book Value per Share = `₹1,200 crore ÷ 10 crore = ₹120`.
3. **Market Price = ₹600:** Because the company grows earnings at 18% annually, investors trade it at **5x Book Value** (`₹600 ÷ ₹120 = 5.0 P/B`), giving it a Market Cap of `₹6,000 crore`.
4. **If ABC declares a 50% dividend:** You receive **₹5 per share** (`50% of ₹10 Face Value`), which is a **0.83% dividend yield** (`₹5 ÷ ₹600 Market Price`).

---

## Key Takeaways

* **Never confuse Face Value percentage with Market Price yield:** Always convert percentage dividend announcements into rupee terms using **Face Value**, then divide by **Market Price** to find your true dividend yield.
* **Book Value is an accounting anchor, not a price ceiling:** Asset-light, high-ROE businesses routinely trade at several times their Book Value, while banks and NBFCs are closely judged on P/B multiples.
* **Stock splits change Face Value and Market Price proportionally:** A split multiplies your share count and divides both Face Value and Market Price—leaving your total wealth and Book Value ownership percentage unchanged.
