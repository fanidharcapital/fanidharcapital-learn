Welcome to **Lesson 76** of **Module 03: Fundamental Analysis** (**Lessons 61–90**) in the **Shree Fanidhar Capital Educational Series**.

In **Lesson 75**, we examined **Return Ratios (`ROE`, `ROCE`, `ROIC`) and DuPont Analysis** to measure how efficiently capital compounds. High returns mean little if a business runs out of near-term cash to pay suppliers and lenders or gets crushed by debt interest during an economic slowdown. Today, we focus on **Liquidity & Solvency Ratios**—the balance-sheet health checks that tell you whether a company can survive rough weather.

---

## 1. Liquidity vs. Solvency: Two Distinct Survival Lenses

* **Liquidity** measures a company's ability to meet **short-term obligations** (`within 12 months`) using liquid or near-term assets—without having to distress-sell core assets or scramble for emergency credit.
* **Solvency** measures a company's ability to meet **long-term debt and fixed interest commitments** over multi-year business cycles.

| Ratio | Category | Formula | What It Tells You |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **1. Current Ratio** | Short-Term Liquidity | $\frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$ | Broad cushion of assets maturing/converting to cash within 12 months relative to short-term dues. |
| **2. Quick Ratio (`Acid-Test`)** | Short-Term Liquidity | $\frac{\text{Cash \& Cash Equivalents} + \text{Trade Receivables}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$ *(or $\frac{\text{Current Assets} - \text{Inventory}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$)* | Stricter liquidity check excluding **inventory**, which may be slow-moving or hard to liquidate quickly. |
| **3. Debt-to-Equity (`D/E`)** | Long-Term Solvency | $\frac{\text{Total Debt}}{\text{Shareholders’ Equity}}$ | Proportion of debt financing vs. equity cushion (`Net Debt / Equity` is even sharper when cash is large). |
| **4. Interest Coverage Ratio (`ICR`)** | Long-Term Solvency | $\frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest Expense (Finance Costs)}}$ | How many times operating profit covers annual interest obligations. |

## 2. Worked Comparison: Conservative Balance Sheet vs. Stretched Leverage (`₹ Crore`)

| Metric | Firm A (`Conservative`) | Firm B (`Leveraged`) | Analytical Takeaway |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **Cash & Bank Balances** | `₹120 Cr` | `₹15 Cr` | Strong cash buffer in Firm A |
| **Trade Receivables** | `₹180 Cr` | `₹135 Cr` | — |
| **Inventory** | `₹150 Cr` | `₹250 Cr` | Firm B holds heavy inventory (`50%` of current assets) |
| **Total Current Assets** | **`₹450 Cr`** | **`₹400 Cr`** | — |
| **Total Current Liabilities** | **`₹250 Cr`** | **`₹320 Cr`** | — |
| **Current Ratio** | **`1.80x`** | **`1.25x`** | Firm A has a wider short-term cushion |
| **Quick Ratio** *(excl. Inventory)* | **`1.20x`** (`300 / 250`) | **`0.47x`** (`150 / 320`) | Firm B relies heavily on selling inventory to meet near-term dues |
| **Total Debt / Shareholders’ Equity** | **`0.20x`** (`₹100 Cr / ₹500 Cr`) | **`1.80x`** (`₹540 Cr / ₹300 Cr`) | Firm B carries much higher financial risk |
| **`EBIT` / Interest Expense (`ICR`)** | **`18.0x`** (`₹180 Cr / ₹10 Cr`) | **`2.2x`** (`₹121 Cr / ₹55 Cr`) | A `35%` drop in `EBIT` would squeeze Firm B's interest cushion tightly |

## 3. Reading Ratios With Context—Not Rigid Rules of Thumb

Mechanical cutoffs can mislead if you ignore how a business actually operates:

1. **Why Negative Working Capital Can Be a Strength:** Capital-efficient retailers and `FMCG` companies often collect cash upfront from customers while paying suppliers on 30–60 day credit terms. Their **Current Ratio** can look below `1.0x` even while cash flow is rock-solid—verify via `CFO` and working-capital days (`Lesson 69`).
2. **Low `Debt-to-Equity` Is Not Always Risk-Free:** A company with `D/E = 0.4x` can still face trouble if its debt is **short-term** and maturing within 6 months while `CFO` is weak, or if it has large off-balance-sheet guarantees (`Lesson 73`).
3. **Interest Coverage Under Stress:** An `ICR` of `6x–10x+` gives comfortable breathing room; an `ICR` below `2x` leaves little buffer if operating margins dip or interest rates rise. *(Remember: for **Banks and NBFCs**, debt is raw material—evaluate them using `CAR/CRAR`, `GNPA/NNPA`, and `NIM` instead of industrial `D/E` or `ICR`.)*

---

## Key Takeaways

* **Liquidity ratios (`Current Ratio` and `Quick Ratio`)** test near-term cash readiness, while **solvency ratios (`Debt-to-Equity` and `Interest Coverage`)** test long-term debt durability.
* **Quick Ratio strips out inventory**, giving a tougher test of short-term ability to cover current liabilities without forced inventory sales.
* **Always cross-check balance-sheet ratios against `CFO`, debt maturity schedules, and business model norms** before drawing conclusions.
